Group exploration

Choose a path. Make the thinking visible.

Try one activity in each eight-minute round. Keep the shared goal, rotate roles, and save one classroom idea.

Review and recall

Round one: Bingo. Round two: Quiz and Flashcard Studio.

Ask everyone to retrieve before comparing answers.

Puzzles and reasoning

Round one: Meme Puzzle. Round two: Fortune Wheel.

After a reveal or solved phrase, ask for evidence and an example.

Create and explain

Round one: Story Wheel. Round two: Dicebreaker Creator.

Explain a sequence, then use a follow-up to extend an idea.

Our exploration routine

  1. One quiet minute: everyone predicts or drafts an answer.
  2. Four minutes: try the shared task; change the person using the device.
  3. Two minutes: explain, ask a follow-up, and check the reasoning.
  4. One minute: record a classroom idea and one piece of evidence.

Round one · minutes eighteen to twenty-six

Round one

Bingo Card Generator and Bingo Caller

Why try it? Review and explain a concept before marking it.

Paste the eight workshop terms into a three-by-three card with a free center. Read a clue, let each person decide, then ask a teammate to explain why the term fits before marking.

Make or share: One marked concept with an explanation everyone can give.

Listen for: An explanation that connects the clue to the concept, rather than a lucky match.

Round one

Meme Puzzle

Why try it? Use a puzzle reveal to invite explanations.

Load the workshop question-and-answer CSV or choose a library activity. Before revealing a tile, ask one person to answer and another to explain or question the reasoning. Use the image as a conversation starter, not proof of understanding.

Make or share: One content explanation and one question worth discussing.

Listen for: Reasoning about the content, beyond excitement about the hidden image.

Round one

Story Wheel

Why try it? Explain the order and importance of events.

Use the three-event seed-growing example. Choose the events, put them in order, and explain why the sequence makes sense. Try a different order and ask which explanation would need to change.

Make or share: A three-event wheel and a spoken explanation of its order.

Listen for: A connection between events, not just a list.

Round two · minutes twenty-six to thirty-four

Round two

Quiz and Flashcard Studio

Why try it? Recall first, compare reasoning, then use feedback.

Import the workshop quiz CSV. Each person answers silently before the group compares reasoning. Reveal the explanation, then choose one item for another try. The current tool also uses the QuizSplat name.

Make or share: A revised answer and a reason for the revision.

Listen for: Each person recalls before seeing a teammate’s answer.

Round two

Dicebreaker Creator

Why try it? Give everyone a turn to elaborate and reflect.

Paste one workshop prompt into each of the six face fields in the creator. Roll a prompt, allow quiet thinking, then invite a response and a follow-up from someone else. Let people pass, write, or speak through a partner.

Make or share: One explanation extended by a colleague’s follow-up.

Listen for: An example or connection that adds meaning to the first answer.

Round two

Fortune Wheel

Why try it? Use phrase recall as a starting point for meaning.

Paste the workshop phrases into a custom puzzle set. After solving a phrase, explain it with a classroom example. Rotate the person proposing an answer so one confident voice does not take every turn.

Make or share: A solved phrase plus a classroom example.

Listen for: A useful example; solving letters alone does not show understanding.

Read the evidence with care.

Structured group tasks need a common purpose, supported participation, and attention to who contributes. The EEF review distinguishes this from simply sitting together. We use peer explanations and checks to surface reasoning, not to claim that any tool automatically produces deeper learning.

The IES practice guide discusses retrieval and explanatory questions. These short conference activities illustrate routines; they do not establish learning gains or numerical effects for SplatWorks tools.
