Presenter guide

High-Impact Teaching Math Tools Without the Price Tag

A facilitation companion for a room of approximately 100 people in rows. Open a public activity in another tab to project; answers remain closed until revealed.

Session elapsed00:00Suggested total: 50 minutes
Five segments · 50 minutes total

0–5 min · 5 minutes

Which graph tells the truth?

Both graphs show Garden A: 12 insects and Garden B: 15 insects. Which graph exaggerates the visual difference?

Audience routine: Think → vote → explain → check → revise. Stay seated; devices optional.

Open public activity ↗Open demonstration ↗
Open facilitation notes and answers

Lead: Give 20 seconds of silent thinking. Ask for one finger for Graph A or two for Graph B. Invite a 30-second neighbor explanation, then reveal the interpretation.

Answer / teaching point: Graph B exaggerates the difference. Its vertical axis begins at 10. Garden B has 3 more insects, or 25% more than Garden A—not 2.5 times as many. A zero baseline preserves bar-length comparisons of totals.

Strategies: Teacher clarity, Argumentation, Corrective feedback

Transition: The data stayed the same. The representation changed what we noticed. Next, make number relationships visible before asking students to calculate.

5–15 min · 10 minutes

Make mathematics visible

Start at 4 on a number line and move 3 equal jumps to the right. Where do you land?

Audience routine: Think → vote → explain → check → revise. Stay seated; devices optional.

Open public activity ↗Open demonstration ↗
Open facilitation notes and answers

Lead: State the goal: explain addition as movement. Model the start at 4, predict, and reveal one jump at a time. Contrast counting the starting point as the first jump. Ask a neighbor to explain why there are 3 intervals, not 3 visited numbers.

Answer / teaching point: You land on 7: 4 → 5 → 6 → 7. The starting number is not a jump. Use objects or a drawing before the number line if needed.

Strategies: Teacher clarity, Direct instruction, Corrective feedback

Transition: An accurate representation supports an accurate explanation. Now ask which representation fits a data question.

15–35 min · 20 minutes

Investigate with GraphSplat

Which representation best shows how the same campus location changes temperature from 9 a.m. to noon to 3 p.m.?

Audience routine: Think → vote → explain → check → revise. Stay seated; devices optional.

Open public activity ↗Open demonstration ↗
Open facilitation notes and answers

Lead: Spend 7 minutes on the garden scale comparison, 6 minutes choosing a representation, and 7 minutes extending to a lesson. Launch GraphSplat and enter the garden CSV. Keep the local examples ready if Wi-Fi fails. Ask only one or two whole-room explanations.

Answer / teaching point: A line graph is useful for this question because the observations are ordered in time. Bars can compare readings, but a line emphasizes change. A pie chart suggests parts of a whole; a scatter plot is useful for relationships between paired numerical variables.

Strategies: Argumentation, Mathematical problem solving, Corrective feedback, Transfer strategies

Transition: Close the examples. What can we retrieve about choosing and interpreting graphs?

35–45 min · 10 minutes

Check what sticks

Recall first. Explain your answer. Then check and revise.

Audience routine: Think → vote → explain → check → revise. Stay seated; devices optional.

Open public activity ↗Open demonstration ↗
Open facilitation notes and answers

Lead: Give about 45 seconds per question: silent recall, optional fingers or private note, neighbor explanation, reveal, revision. Use the remaining time to demonstrate how to build a similar recall set in Quiz and Flashcard Studio. Revisit with a different dataset in a later lesson.

Answer / teaching point: 1: A bar-chart baseline changes apparent bar-length ratios. 2: A line graph helps show change over time. 3: Explain 4 + 3 by three jumps from 4, ending at 7.

Strategies: Retrieval practice, Corrective feedback

Transition: A correct choice is a starting point. Plan how you will elicit and respond to explanations tomorrow.

45–50 min · 5 minutes

ACE your math lesson

Articulate a strategy, connect it to a tool, and extend it to your own mathematical concept.

Audience routine: Think → vote → explain → check → revise. Stay seated; devices optional.

Open public activity ↗Open demonstration ↗
Open facilitation notes and answers

Lead: Give 2 minutes for an individual plan, 1 minute to share with a neighbor, and 2 minutes for a final revision and resource links. Invite a plan on paper or in the browser. No collection of responses.

Answer / teaching point: A useful plan names a mathematical goal, a strategy, a tool serving that goal, evidence of understanding, and a next task in a new context.

Strategies: Transfer strategies, Teacher clarity

Transition: Open the resource library and download the packet. The activities remain available after the convention.
