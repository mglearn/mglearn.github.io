Experience 1 / 5 · 0–5 minutes

Which graph tells the truth?

Both graphs show Garden A: 12 insects and Garden B: 15 insects. Which graph exaggerates the visual difference?

Which graph exaggerates the visual difference?

Check explanation

Graph B exaggerates the difference. Its vertical axis begins at 10. Garden B has 3 more insects, or 25% more than Garden A—not 2.5 times as many. A zero baseline preserves bar-length comparisons of totals.

Explain to a neighbor: Which labels support your claim? Why do bar-chart baselines matter?

Strategies: Teacher clarity · Argumentation · Corrective feedback

Experience 2 / 5 · 5–15 minutes

Make mathematics visible

Start at 4 on a number line and move 3 equal jumps to the right. Where do you land?

Where do three jumps from 4 land?
Need a hint?

Count the spaces you travel, not the starting number.

Check explanation

You land on 7: 4 → 5 → 6 → 7. The starting number is not a jump. Use objects or a drawing before the number line if needed.

Model → misconception → revision

Model 4 → 5 → 6 → 7. Then ask why counting 4 as the first jump gives a different answer. Invite a corrected explanation using objects, fingers, a drawing, or the number line.

Adapt it: Younger learners can model joining objects; older learners can use negative starts, fractions, or variable changes. Check the grade-level goal first.

Strategies: Teacher clarity · Direct instruction · Corrective feedback

Experience 3 / 5 · 15–35 minutes

Investigate with GraphSplat

Which representation best shows how the same campus location changes temperature from 9 a.m. to noon to 3 p.m.?

A. Revisit the garden evidence

Which display preserves the bar-length comparison of totals?

Check explanation

Graph A. A zero baseline makes the bar lengths proportional to 12 and 15. Garden B has 3 more insects, or 25% more. Graph B labels its axis, but its visible lengths above 10 have a ratio of 5 to 2. Truncation can be appropriate for some line graphs; the concern here is comparing bar lengths.

B. Choose a representation for the question

Same location, same day: 9 a.m. = 18°C; noon = 24°C; 3 p.m. = 27°C. Question: How does the temperature change over time?

Which representation best emphasizes this change?
Need a hint?

Look for an ordered sequence in time. Ask whether the values are parts of a whole.

Check explanation

A line graph is useful for this question because the observations are ordered in time. Bars can compare readings, but a line emphasizes change. A pie chart suggests parts of a whole; a scatter plot is useful for relationships between paired numerical variables.

A question changes the choice.

Compare garden categories with bars. Track a plant’s height across days with a line. Show categories that exhaust a whole with a pie. Investigate paired numerical variables, such as sunlight hours and height, with a scatter plot. Association alone does not establish causation.

C. Extend to a real investigation

Choose schoolyard biodiversity, campus temperatures, growth over time, or a linear relationship. Decide what you will measure, how you will sample fairly, and which display answers the question. Equal sampling effort matters when comparing gardens.

Experience 4 / 5 · 35–45 minutes

Check what sticks

Recall first. Explain your answer. Then check and revise.

Answer from memory before opening hints or explanations. Use fingers, a private note, a spoken explanation, or these optional controls.

1. What can make a bar chart exaggerate a difference?
Need a hint?

Think about which axis value the bars begin from.

Check explanation

A vertical axis starting above zero can exaggerate bar-length ratios. Read values and units, calculate the actual difference, and inspect the baseline.

2. Which graph helps show change in one location’s temperature over time?
Need a hint?

The observations are ordered by time.

Check explanation

A line graph connects readings in their time order, making change easier to discuss. It does not establish what caused the change.

3. Which explanation models 4 + 3 on a number line?
Need a hint?

Count the intervals traveled.

Check explanation

Start at 4. Make three equal one-unit jumps to 5, 6, and 7. Have the student show the intervals and explain why the starting point is not a jump.

These questions model retrieval and feedback. No responses are collected and no learning-gain score is calculated.

Strategies: Retrieval practice · Corrective feedback
